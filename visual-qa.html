Visual QA without the ticket war

How we redesigned remote Visual QA — not as a checklist, but as a negotiation system between design, QA, engineering, and product.

Situation

«Done» in Jira was not the same as looking like the design.

On a remote product team, Visual QA couldn't mean sitting next to a developer. Design-system consistency across markets mattered — but quality depended on who cared that week. We needed a process that protected product integrity without burning design capacity or developer flow.

"This wasn't a Figma problem. It was a negotiation problem."

Two experiments

Both extremes were half-right.

We tried two approaches. Each solved one side of the conflict and broke the other.

Attempt A

Sync Visual QA

  • Live screen-share: Inspect Element ↔ Figma
  • High integrity within one feature story
  • Related fixes shipped together
  • Broke developer deep work
  • Many finds = DS consistency — low eng priority

Attempt B

Async ticket factory

  • Fit eng async culture
  • Batchable, prioritizable work
  • Design drowned writing tickets
  • Priority killed «small» consistency fixes
  • Surface integrity fragmented

Hybrid pipeline

Keep what worked. Gate what’s expensive.

  • QA absorbs coarse visual bugs via existing ticket habit
  • Short designer–QA sync replaces long dev sessions
  • AI drafts residual tickets — designers own severity
  • Live dev QA becomes PO-gated premium mode

Two extremes. One hybrid that kept the best of each.

Hidden actor

QA engineers already ran functional testing — and often caught coarse visual bugs the same way they file functional defects. Developers were used to that ticket stream. The mountain of tickets wasn't only a design burden — it was a habit we could redirect.

The conflict

Three valid goals. Zero pure winners.

Design wanted coherent surfaces and design-system fidelity. Engineering wanted protected focus and clear batchable work. QA needed coverage without inventing a new religion.

We sat down with QA, developers, and the Product Owner — and redesigned the sequence, not the tool.

Three forces. One negotiated pipeline.

The hybrid

A pipeline of filters — not one ritual for everything.

Default mode is lean and async. Live Visual QA with developers becomes a premium mode — justified by product, not designer preference.

01

Functional QA pass

QA territory — including gross visual issues. Tickets in the format engineering already trusts.

02

Short sync: Designer ↔ QA

Triage what's left. Cheaper than pulling every developer into a live review.

03

Residual designer tickets

Only fine-grained design-system issues. AI drafts the tickets — designers still own severity and accuracy.

04

Live Visual QA — PO-gated

Only when the Product Owner marks the story: must look right for product consistency. Same story, related fixes, justified interruption.

Mental model

QA ticket habit = first filter · Designer–QA sync = second filter · Designer tickets = residual · Dev live sync = premium mode, PO-gated · AI = writing acceleration, not judgment.

Result

Less interrupt. Less ticket hell. Surfaces that mattered stayed coherent.

QA first filter
Coarse visual absorbed by existing ticket habit
PO-gated sync
Live designer–dev sessions became the exception

Design ticket volume dropped because QA absorbed the coarse layer. Engineering interruption dropped because live sessions became rare and justified. Quality didn't collapse into «QA only» — designers still owned fine design-system judgment.

The process became shared know-how: design, QA, engineering, and product agreed on the sequence — not a design decree.

What I learned

Process design is product design for the organisation.

  • 01Don't invent rituals — redirect habits partners already trust.
  • 02«Perfect QA» that destroys partner flow fails. Integrity without dignity is temporary.
  • 03Put expensive modes behind a product gate — the PO, not designer preference.
  • 04Sequence matters: functional → coarse visual → fine visual.
  • 05AI accelerates writing residual tickets. Humans still decide what matters.
