Visual QA without the ticket war

How we redesigned remote Visual QA — not as a checklist, but as a negotiation system between design, QA, engineering, and product.

Situation

«Done» in Jira was not the same as looking like the design.

On a remote product team, Visual QA couldn't mean sitting next to a developer. Design-system consistency across markets mattered — but quality depended on who cared that week. We needed a process that protected product integrity without burning design capacity or developer flow.

"This wasn't a Figma problem. It was a negotiation problem."

Two experiments

Both extremes were half-right.

We tried two approaches. Each solved one side of the conflict and broke the other.

Attempt A

Sync Visual QA

  • Live screen-share: implementation ↔ Figma
  • High visual integrity within one feature story
  • Related fixes could ship together
  • Interrupted developer deep work
  • Design-system fixes competed with higher-priority engineering work

Attempt B

Async ticket factory

  • Fit engineering's async culture
  • Made work batchable and prioritizable
  • Design spent too much time writing tickets
  • "Small" consistency fixes lost priority
  • Related visual fixes became fragmented
Attempt A

Sync Visual QA

Live screen-share: high integrity per feature, but broke developer deep work and couldn’t scale beyond one story.

Attempt B

Async ticket factory

Fit eng culture, but design drowned writing tickets. Priority killed consistency fixes. Surface integrity fragmented.

Hybrid pipeline

Keep what worked. Gate what’s expensive.

  • QA absorbs coarse visual bugs via existing ticket habit
  • Short designer–QA sync replaces long dev sessions
  • AI drafts residual tickets — designers own severity
  • Live dev QA becomes PO-gated premium mode

Two extremes. One hybrid that kept the best of each.

Hidden actor

QA engineers already ran functional testing — and often caught coarse visual bugs through the same ticket workflow. Developers trusted that stream. Rather than adding another parallel review ritual, we could build on an existing habit.

The conflict

Three valid goals. Zero pure winners.

Design wanted coherent surfaces and design-system fidelity. Engineering wanted protected focus and clear, batchable work. QA needed reliable coverage without a separate visual-testing bureaucracy.

We sat down with QA, developers, and the Product Owner — and redesigned the sequence, not the tool.

Three forces. One negotiated pipeline.

The hybrid

A pipeline of filters — not one ritual for everything.

Default mode is lean and async. Live Visual QA with developers becomes a premium mode — justified by product needs, not designer preference.

01

Functional QA + coarse visual checks

QA checks behaviour and obvious visual defects, then files actionable tickets in the format engineering already trusts. Design is kept in the loop rather than starting a separate stream for the same findings.

02

Short sync: Designer ↔ QA

Review QA findings, remove duplicates, and separate coarse defects from fine design-system issues. Resolve design ambiguity before adding residual tickets; escalate priority decisions to the PO.

03

Residual designer tickets

Designers inspect the remaining fine-grained issues and document actionable fixes. AI assists with ticket drafts; designers own accuracy and severity, while the PO owns delivery priority. Engineering works from the documented, prioritised ticket stream, and fixes are verified.

04

Live Visual QA — PO-gated

Optional escalation, not a mandatory final meeting. When visual consistency materially matters to the story, the PO authorises a live designer-developer review so related fixes can be resolved together.

Ownership stays explicit

QA owns functional coverage and coarse visual findings. Design owns fine visual judgment and design-system fidelity. Engineering owns implementation. The PO prioritises bugs against OKRs and engineering capacity. Findings, decisions, and deferred work remain documented.

Outcome

A shared quality process, not competing review streams.

The hybrid pipeline was adopted as shared team practice. QA handled the coarse layer, designers retained fine design-system judgment, and the PO decided which fixes mattered now — including when a live review was worth the interruption.

Design ticket volume dropped as QA absorbed coarse visual findings. Live designer-developer sessions became the exception rather than the default. These were qualitative process outcomes; the effort estimates below are a separate model, not rollout measurements.

"The goal wasn't fewer quality checks. It was less duplicated effort and clearer decisions."

Making the tradeoffs explicit

Illustrative estimate · not measured results

What could this save on one cross-platform story?

I modelled a small dashboard-control story spanning three weeks and delivered across iOS, Android, and web. The scenario uses a team of two designers, two QA engineers, and three platform developers, with one developer per platform.

Hours are summed across the people doing the work — not multiplied by every team member. A one-hour designer-developer call therefore costs two person-hours.

The estimated saving is 7.4 person-hours per story against live review, or 4.4 against independent tickets. It comes from less correction work and shorter follow-up — not from removing designer ownership.

View assumptions and calculation

Scope and baseline

  • One small story, three platforms, one developer per platform. Design and QA work is allocated across their respective two-person teams.
  • Four hours of visual corrections per platform is the midpoint of the assumed three-to-five-hour range.
  • Each platform is counted separately. Shared cross-platform findings are not credited with extra savings.
  • Initial design, implementation, functional bug fixes, and existing PO work are outside the model. QA technical-check time is included and held constant.

Live-review workflow · per platform

Design: 1 hour initial live review + 1 hour final review = 2 hours. Engineering: 1 hour initial review + 4 hours corrections + 1 hour final review and small fixes = 6 hours.

Independent tickets · per platform

Design: 1 hour inspection and ticket creation + 1 hour final review = 2 hours. Engineering: 4 hours corrections + 1 hour final review and small fixes = 5 hours.

QA in both baselines · per platform

1 hour technical checks + 30 minutes coarse visual checks + 30 minutes ticket creation and clarification + 30 minutes verification = 2.5 hours.

Hybrid model · per platform

Design: 15 minutes QA triage + 1 hour deep visual inspection and residual tickets + 30 minutes final review = 1.75 hours.

Engineering: 15 minutes ticket review and clarification + 3.2 hours visual corrections + 30 minutes final review and small fixes = 3.95 hours.

QA: 1 hour technical checks + 30 minutes coarse visual checks + 15 minutes ticket creation + 15 minutes designer triage + 15 minutes verification = 2.25 hours.

PO: 15 additional minutes of prioritisation for the whole story, not per platform.

Where the assumed saving comes from

Correction effort is assumed to fall by 20%: 4 × 0.8 = 3.2 hours per platform. This represents removing duplicate requests, batching related fixes, and agreeing current scope. Ticket creation and QA verification are assumed to shorten, while final review falls from one hour to 30 minutes.

No additional saving is assigned to AI drafting. The designer's deep inspection and residual-ticket work remains one hour per platform.

Arithmetic

Live review: (2 + 6 + 2.5) × 3 = 31.5 person-hours. Independent tickets: (2 + 5 + 2.5) × 3 = 28.5 person-hours. Hybrid: (1.75 + 3.95 + 2.25) × 3 + 0.25 = 24.1 person-hours.

Total reduction: (31.5 − 24.1) ÷ 31.5 = 23.5%, rounded to 24%. Engineering reduction: (18 − 11.85) ÷ 18 = 34.2%, rounded to 34%.

Against independent tickets: (28.5 − 24.1) ÷ 28.5 = 15.4% total reduction; (15 − 11.85) ÷ 15 = 21% engineering reduction.

What the model does not prove

The model includes a 30-minute final designer-developer review on every platform as a conservative allowance. The adopted workflow only uses live reviews when PO-gated; no further saving from that policy is claimed here.

The 20% correction reduction and shorter follow-up are assumptions, not observed metrics. Deferred fixes remain backlog work, not eliminated defects. Person-hours do not establish calendar-time savings, faster releases, or fewer escaped defects. The three-week story duration is context, not the denominator for these percentages.

What I learned

Process design is product design for the organisation.

  1. 01Don't invent rituals — redirect habits partners already trust.
  2. 02"Perfect QA" that destroys partner flow fails. Integrity without dignity is temporary.
  3. 03Put expensive modes behind a product gate — the PO, not designer preference.
  4. 04Sequence matters: functional → coarse visual → fine visual. Document decisions, not just defects.
  5. 05AI accelerates writing residual tickets. Humans still decide what matters.
